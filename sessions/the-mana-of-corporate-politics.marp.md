# The Mana of Corporate Politics

<p class="lead">A practical guide to political capital without becoming a workplace supervillain</p>

<span class="pill">Trust</span>
<span class="pill">Influence</span>
<span class="pill">Judgment</span>
<span class="pill">Long Game</span>

> You already know the system. You may just need a better character sheet.

<!--
Timing: 0:00 to 1:45 | 1 minute 45 seconds

Talk track:

I was explaining corporate politics to a mentee recently. I could see the phrase itself creating resistance. "Corporate politics" sounds like secret alliances, strategic flattery, and someone carrying a suspiciously elaborate org chart.

I started to reach for the usual phrases: stakeholder management, executive presence, influence without authority. All accurate, but none especially illuminating.

Then I remembered that we work in tech. Tech people are nerds. So I said, "Think of it as a mana-based spell-casting system."

That got us somewhere.

You have a pool of resources. Actions cost resources. Some actions refill the pool. If you spend more than you have, bad things happen. Suddenly, a fuzzy organizational concept had mechanics.

This talk is not about manipulating people or winning every meeting. It is about understanding how trust, credibility, timing, and power affect whether an action works.

The goal is not to become political. The goal is to navigate organizations deliberately, ethically, and with fewer avoidable fireballs.

Transition: Let us begin with the basic game mechanics.
-->

## The Core System

<div class="columns">
<div class="card">

### Mana

**Political capital**

- Trust
- Credibility
- Goodwill
- Relationships
- Track record

</div>
<div class="card">

### Spells

**Actions that spend capital**

- Push back
- Challenge
- Escalate
- Advocate
- Ask for change

</div>
</div>

<div class="meter"><div class="meter-fill"></div></div>

<p class="small">The balance is invisible, contextual, and very real.</p>

<!--
Timing: 1:45 to 3:00 | 1 minute 15 seconds

Talk track:

Here is the model.

Mana is political capital. It consists of the trust, credibility, and goodwill you have accumulated with peers, managers, stakeholders, and leaders.

Nobody gives you a dashboard showing a balance of 72 percent. The meter is invisible, and different people may estimate it differently. Yet it strongly affects what you can do.

Spells are actions that rely on that capital. Publicly challenging a plan is a spell. Escalating a risk is a spell. Asking a leader to reverse a decision is definitely a spell.

The key insight is not that influence exists. Most of us already know that. The useful insight is that actions have different costs depending on the caster, audience, timing, history, and organizational culture.

A trusted engineer asking a difficult question may be heard as protecting the system. A new engineer asking the same question may be treated as lacking context. That difference is not always fair, but pretending it does not exist does not protect anyone.

Transition: Before we continue the metaphor, we need an ethical boundary.
-->

## Influence Is Not Manipulation

| Healthy influence | Cynical politicking |
|---|---|
| Makes concerns understandable | Hides the real objective |
| Builds genuine relationships | Treats people as transactions |
| Shares credit | Hoards credit |
| Uses evidence and judgment | Uses fear, rumor, or pressure |
| Seeks better outcomes | Seeks advantage at any cost |

> **Authentic is almost always cheaper than clever.**

<!--
Timing: 3:00 to 4:30 | 1 minute 30 seconds

Talk track:

A quick but important boundary: healthy organizational influence is not the same thing as manipulation.

Healthy influence helps people understand a risk, coordinate around a decision, or improve an outcome. You build real relationships, explain your reasoning, share credit, and state concerns honestly.

Cynical politicking disguises motives, treats relationships as transactions, and tries to win through rumor, pressure, or selective information.

The mana model can describe both behaviors, but description is not endorsement. We are using it to make ethical choices more deliberate, not to teach dark arts with better project management.

One spell from the original model is concealment: the clarifying question that is really a critique, or the "thinking out loud" comment that arrives wearing a complete rebuttal in a trench coat.

People usually notice. If it fails, you spend mana on the concern and lose credibility for hiding it. You paid twice.

If you have a real concern, name it professionally and directly. Authentic is almost always cheaper than clever.

Transition: Ethics matter, and so does recognizing that the game board is not level.
-->

---

## The Map Is Not Fair

- Role and hierarchy change the cost of speaking
- Bias affects who receives credibility
- Marginalized people may pay more for the same action
- Managers can alter the rules for their teams
- Some environments punish candor regardless of skill

<div class="card">
<strong>No individual technique can repair an abusive or unsafe culture.</strong>
Document patterns, seek allies, use formal channels when appropriate, and protect your well-being.
</div>

<!--
Timing: 4:30 to 6:15 | 1 minute 45 seconds

Talk track:

The metaphor has limits because organizations do not offer equal starting stats.

Hierarchy changes spell costs. Bias changes whose expertise is assumed. Identity, employment status, location, tenure, and access to leadership all shape how much political capital someone receives and how quickly they can regenerate it.

Two people can use the same words in the same meeting and receive different reactions. That is not a failure of personal technique. It is often a sign of uneven power.

Managers have an extra responsibility here. You help set the mana costs on your team. If people must spend enormous credibility merely to raise a risk, your process is broken. If only the loudest person can challenge a plan safely, that is not healthy debate.

Some cultures also punish honest disagreement no matter how carefully it is offered. Better phrasing cannot solve retaliation, harassment, discrimination, or sustained psychological unsafety.

In those situations, personal tactics may reduce immediate risk, but they are not the cure. Document patterns. Find trustworthy support. Use formal channels when safe and appropriate. Consider whether staying is worth the cost.

This model should help people see dynamics clearly, never blame them for being harmed by those dynamics.

Transition: With that guardrail in place, let us look at why spells cost different amounts.
-->

## Spell Cost Is Contextual

### Cost = action + audience + timing + history + power

| Lower cost | Higher cost |
|---|---|
| Private question | Public rebuttal |
| Early feedback | Objection after commitment |
| Evidence plus alternative | Block without a path forward |
| Known relationship | First contact during a crisis |
| One stakeholder | Company-wide audience |

> The same sentence can be a cantrip or a boss battle.

<!--
Timing: 6:15 to 7:45 | 1 minute 30 seconds

Talk track:

There is no universal price list.

A private question asked early is usually cheaper than a public objection after leaders have announced a decision. Feedback accompanied by evidence and an alternative costs less than simply blocking the plan.

Audience size matters. So does timing. So does history.

Imagine saying, "I do not think this rollout is safe."

In a design review, backed by test results, that may be a low-cost act of responsible engineering. In an all-hands after launch, with no new evidence, it becomes a much more expensive spell.

Power also changes the equation. A director can sometimes cast a spell almost for free because their role supplies institutional mana. An IC may need stronger evidence or allies to create the same effect.

This does not mean ICs should stay quiet. It means that choosing the venue, timing, and support structure can improve the chance of being heard.

The practical question is not merely, "Am I right?" It is, "What action gives this concern the best chance of producing a useful outcome?"

Transition: Fortunately, some of the strongest actions also happen to be cheap.
-->

## The Best Mana Generators Are Boring

<div class="columns">
<div>

- Deliver on commitments
- Show up prepared
- Close the loop
- Share credit publicly
- Own mistakes privately

</div>
<div>

- Help before you need help
- Do useful, unglamorous work
- Make risks visible early
- Give clear feedback
- Be reliable on small things

</div>
</div>

> The boring stuff is the foundation everything else is built on.

<!--
Timing: 7:45 to 9:30 | 1 minute 45 seconds

Talk track:

How do you regenerate mana? Mostly through the unsexy stuff.

Deliver what you said you would deliver. Show up prepared. Close the loop instead of letting requests dissolve into Slack history. Share credit publicly. Take ownership when things go sideways.

These behaviors create a predictable experience for other people. Predictability becomes trust, and trust becomes political capital.

Publicly championing someone else's work is especially powerful. In the game metaphor, it is a buff. It costs almost nothing and creates value for the person, the team, and everyone watching.

Managers can generate capital by giving air cover, clarifying decisions, and ensuring credit reaches the people who did the work. ICs build it through sound judgment, good execution, and making collaborators more effective.

This is not a transactional checklist where three compliments purchase one escalation. Genuine relationships are not loyalty points.

Think of regeneration as the long-term result of being consistently useful, honest, and generous. People learn that when you speak, you are trying to improve the outcome rather than score a point.

Transition: Take a moment to consider what fills and drains your own pool.
-->

## Quick Reflection: Check Your Mana Bar

### Think of one real decision you may need to influence.

For 45 seconds, ask yourself:

1. **Who needs to trust the message?**
2. **What have I done that creates credibility?**
3. **What could drain that credibility?**
4. **Is there a cheaper, clearer first move?**

<p class="small">No sharing required. Pick something real enough to be useful.</p>

<!--
Timing: 9:30 to 11:00 | 1 minute 30 seconds

Talk track:

Let us make this concrete with a short reflection. No equipment and no mandatory sharing.

Think of one real decision you may need to influence in the next few weeks. It could be an architecture choice, a staffing concern, a deadline, a process change, or a risk nobody wants to discuss.

I will give you about 45 seconds.

Ask yourself four questions. Who needs to trust the message? What have you already done that creates credibility? What behavior or history could drain that credibility? Finally, is there a cheaper and clearer first move than the one you were planning?

Pause for approximately 45 seconds.

Now, hold onto that example.

This exercise is not asking, "How do I make people agree with me?" The point is to choose an action that helps the organization engage with the substance of the concern.

Often, the first move is smaller than we expect. It might be gathering data, speaking privately with an affected peer, or asking the decision owner what evidence would change their mind.

Transition: That brings us to a small set of spells worth recognizing.

## The Practical Spellbook

| Spell | What it looks like | Primary risk |
|---|---|---|
| **Cantrip** | Clarify, suggest, volunteer | Too subtle to change course |
| **Buff** | Champion another person's work | Empty praise feels transactional |
| **Ward** | Pre-brief key stakeholders | Can become secretive if abused |
| **Counterspell** | Block or rebut in the room | Creates defensiveness |
| **AoE** | Reply-all, broad Slack, all-hands | Impact and cost multiply |
| **Summon** | Bring in a senior ally | Burns trust if used too early |
| **Time Stop** | Request data or another review | Repetition looks like stalling |

<p class="small">Choose the smallest spell that can responsibly change the outcome.</p>

<!--
Timing: 11:00 to 12:30 | 1 minute 30 seconds

Talk track:

Here is the concise spellbook.

Cantrips are low-cost actions such as clarifying questions, suggestions, or volunteering for useful work.

Buffs amplify someone else's contribution. Wards are pre-briefs that let stakeholders understand a proposal before the formal decision.

A counterspell blocks or immediately rebuts an idea in the room. It may be necessary, especially when harm is imminent, but it often creates defensiveness.

An area-of-effect spell, or AoE, reaches many stakeholders at once. Think reply-all, a broad Slack channel, or an all-hands question. The radius multiplies both impact and cost.

A summon brings in a senior ally or executive sponsor. It can break a deadlock, but early or repeated summoning can damage working relationships.

Time Stop asks for more data or another review. One use can create space for a better decision. Repeated use looks like stalling because, eventually, it is.

The goal is not to avoid powerful spells. It is to choose the smallest spell that can responsibly change the outcome.

Transition: Let us test that judgment in a realistic delivery scenario.

## Scenario: Friday Deployment Pressure

A product launch is scheduled for Friday.

- Load tests show intermittent data loss
- Product believes the risk is acceptable
- Engineering leadership has already announced the date
- The incident response team has not been consulted
- You have 48 hours

### What do you cast first?

<span class="pill">Counterspell</span>
<span class="pill">AoE</span>
<span class="pill">Ward</span>
<span class="pill">Summon</span>

<!--
Timing: 12:30 to 14:15 | 1 minute 45 seconds

Talk track:

Here is our scenario.

A launch is scheduled for Friday. Load tests show intermittent data loss. Product believes the risk is acceptable. Engineering leadership has already announced the date, and incident response has not been consulted. You have 48 hours.

This works for both ICs and managers because either role may discover the risk, own part of the decision, or need to influence the release.

What do you cast first?

A counterspell means objecting directly in the next launch meeting. An AoE means posting the test results in a broad channel so everyone sees the danger. A ward means privately briefing the product owner, engineering manager, and incident lead before the meeting. A summon means asking a VP or executive sponsor to intervene.

There is no context-free answer. If the defect could cause immediate legal or safety harm, escalation may be necessary now. If there is time to coordinate responsibly, starting with maximum blast radius may create heat without creating clarity.

The most useful default is usually the ward, not because private conversation should hide risk, but because decision makers need a chance to understand the evidence and prepare options.

Transition: Now let us sequence the spells instead of selecting only one.
-->

---

## Scenario: Cast a Sequence, Not a Fireball

1. **Ward:** Pre-brief owners with evidence and impact
2. **Cantrip:** Ask what threshold would delay release
3. **Counterspell:** Object clearly if the unsafe plan remains
4. **Summon:** Escalate to the accountable leader if needed
5. **AoE:** Use broad visibility when scope, urgency, or process requires it

<div class="card">
<strong>IC move:</strong> Bring evidence, user impact, and a viable alternative.<br>
<strong>Manager move:</strong> Create safety to raise the risk, clarify ownership, and absorb escalation cost.
</div>

<!--
Timing: 14:15 to 16:00 | 1 minute 45 seconds

Talk track:

Instead of treating this as a single spell choice, sequence the actions.

First, cast a ward. Pre-brief the product owner, engineering manager, and incident lead. Bring the test evidence, explain the user impact, and identify options such as reducing scope, using a feature flag, or moving the date.

Next, use a cantrip with purpose: "What failure threshold would cause us to delay?" That question exposes whether the team has an actual risk boundary.

If the plan remains unsafe, cast the counterspell clearly. Say, "Based on the reproduced data-loss condition, I recommend we do not release this configuration Friday." Direct does not have to mean dramatic.

If accountable leaders still ignore material risk, summon the appropriate senior owner or formal risk function.

Use AoE when the issue genuinely requires broad visibility, when policy requires documentation, or when private routes are suppressing the risk. Do not use it merely to create social pressure.

For an IC, the job is to bring evidence, impact, and alternatives. For a manager, the job includes creating room for the concern and absorbing some of the political cost. Managers should not make junior staff tank the boss fight alone.

Transition: Whatever sequence you choose, keep an eye on what happens near zero.
-->

## Do Not Go Negative

<div class="columns">
<div class="card">

### At zero

- You spent your available capital
- Recovery is still possible
- Pause and let outcomes speak

</div>
<div class="card">

### Below zero

- People stop listening
- Motives replace substance
- Invitations disappear
- Good ideas receive less consideration

</div>
</div>

> Going to zero is fine. Going negative is where it gets dangerous.

<!--
Timing: 16:00 to 17:30 | 1 minute 30 seconds

Talk track:

The danger zone is not spending mana. The danger is spending past zero without recognizing it.

Suppose you challenge a decision and the room engages. That may be expensive but worthwhile. If you continue repeating the same objection after the decision owner has acknowledged it, you may spend more capital without adding information.

Going negative rarely produces a dramatic notification. Instead, people stop listening. They interpret your motives before considering your evidence. You are invited later, or not at all. Even strong ideas receive less attention.

This can be unfair, and sometimes the person raising the concern is being punished for telling the truth. The response is not always to become quieter. In high-stakes situations, documentation or escalation may matter more than preserving capital.

For ordinary disagreements, however, notice when you have made the point. Ask how the decision will be documented. Clarify ownership. Then stop casting long enough for the outcome to generate new evidence.

Going to zero is fine. Going negative is where it gets dangerous.

Transition: One skill helps you avoid unnecessary spending while still responding to what is really happening.

## True Sight: Read the Actual Situation

When tension appears, ask:

- Is this a **content** disagreement or a **status** threat?
- Is the decision open, or are we performing consultation?
- Who owns the outcome?
- What evidence could change the decision?
- Is someone casting on me?
- Does this require response, documentation, or distance?

> Respond to the actual situation, not only the surface conversation.

<!--
Timing: 17:30 to 19:00 | 1 minute 30 seconds

Talk track:

True Sight is the passive skill of recognizing what is actually happening.

A technical disagreement may really be a status threat. A request for feedback may arrive after the decision is effectively closed. A clarifying question may be a concealed objection. A demand for more data may be a legitimate safety check, or it may be Time Stop.

Before reacting, ask whether the conflict concerns content, ownership, status, timing, or trust.

Then ask who owns the outcome and what evidence could change the decision. If nobody can answer, the meeting may not be a decision-making forum at all.

True Sight also includes recognizing when someone is casting on you. That does not mean assigning sinister motives to every awkward comment. It means noticing patterns and choosing a proportionate response.

Sometimes the right action is to answer directly. Sometimes it is to document the concern. Sometimes it is to ask for a private follow-up. In a harmful pattern, the right action may be to create distance and seek support.

Respond to the actual situation, not only the surface conversation.

Transition: That awareness supports a much longer strategy than winning today's meeting.
-->

## Play the Long Game

### Mana stacking is not hoarding.

- Build trust before the urgent request
- Spend capital on outcomes that matter
- Let successful judgment rebuild the pool
- Skip fights that produce no meaningful change
- Make other people more effective

> The people with massive mana pools did not hoard them.

<!--
Timing: 19:00 to 20:30 | 1 minute 30 seconds

Talk track:

The long game is mana stacking: becoming good at building capital so you can spend it when something important needs to change.

That does not mean hoarding political capital forever. Capital has value because you can use it.

People with large mana pools often spend them. They advocate for someone who is not in the room. They challenge a bad deadline. They protect a team from chaotic priorities. When those actions produce good outcomes, their credibility returns stronger.

Timing matters. You do not have to cast every time something bothers you. Some issues are preferences wearing little risk costumes.

Save the expensive spells for decisions with meaningful impact. When you do act, make the concern specific, connect it to shared goals, and propose a path forward.

The best long-term strategy also makes other people more effective. Share context. Sponsor good ideas. Credit contributors. Help someone else avoid a mistake.

Influence grows when people see that your involvement improves both decisions and working relationships.

Transition: The model also creates concrete responsibilities for each role.

## ICs and Managers: Different Character Classes

| Individual contributors | Managers |
|---|---|
| Build credibility through judgment and delivery | Set the cost of candor on the team |
| Bring evidence and alternatives | Give air cover for responsible dissent |
| Choose venue and timing deliberately | Share context and decision boundaries |
| Document material risks | Prevent retaliation and credit theft |
| Build peer relationships before a crisis | Spend positional capital for the team |

<div class="card">
<strong>Shared objective:</strong> Better decisions without making honesty unnecessarily expensive.
</div>

<!--
Timing: 20:30 to 22:00 | 1 minute 30 seconds

Talk track:

ICs and managers play different character classes.

ICs often build capital through delivery, technical judgment, and collaboration. When challenging a decision, they can improve the odds by bringing evidence, naming impact, and offering alternatives. They should also document material risks when appropriate.

Managers have positional mana in addition to personal credibility. That creates extra responsibility.

Managers set much of the cost of candor. If someone raises a concern, do you ask for evidence and engage with it, or do you label the person negative? Do you clarify which decisions are open for input, or invite debate after everything is settled?

Good managers give air cover for responsible dissent, share context, prevent retaliation, and ensure credit lands correctly. They spend positional capital so the team does not have to spend personal capital on every systemic issue.

Both groups share the same objective: better decisions without making honesty unnecessarily expensive.

This is not only kinder. It improves risk detection, delivery quality, retention, and organizational learning.

## Before You Cast

### Pause and check:

1. **Purpose:** What outcome am I protecting?
2. **Cost:** How much capital might this spend?
3. **Target:** Who can actually change the outcome?
4. **Timing:** Is this the right moment and venue?
5. **Form:** Can I start with a cheaper honest spell?
6. **Boundary:** Is the environment unsafe or acting in bad faith?

> The move is not to never spend mana. The move is to choose deliberately.

<!--
Timing: 22:00 to 23:30 | 1 minute 30 seconds

Talk track:

Before casting, run a short check.

First, purpose. What outcome are you protecting? If the answer is mostly "I want everyone to know I was right," take another pass.

Second, cost. How much capital might this action spend, and who else could pay the price?

Third, target. Speak to someone who can change the outcome. Venting broadly can feel active while accomplishing very little.

Fourth, timing. Is this early enough to influence the decision? Is the venue appropriate for the sensitivity and urgency?

Fifth, form. Can you begin with a cheaper but still honest spell, such as a private pre-brief, a request for decision criteria, or an evidence-backed alternative?

Finally, boundary. Is the environment engaging in good faith? If the pattern includes retaliation, discrimination, or concealed decisions, stop treating it as a normal persuasion problem. Shift toward documentation, formal support, or protecting your exit options.

The move is not to never spend mana. The move is to know what you are spending and choose deliberately.

## Summary and Key Takeaways

- **Mana is political capital:** trust, credibility, and goodwill
- **Spells are actions:** each has a different contextual cost
- **Regeneration is mostly boring:** reliability, generosity, follow-through
- **Authenticity beats concealment:** name the real concern
- **Zero is survivable:** negative is the danger zone
- **Use power responsibly:** managers should lower the cost of candor
- **The map is uneven:** individual tactics cannot fix harmful systems
- **Play the long game:** spend capital on outcomes that matter

<!--
Timing: 23:30 to 24:30 | 1 minute

Talk track:

Here is the model in one minute.

Mana is political capital: trust, credibility, goodwill, relationships, and a track record of judgment.

Spells are actions that rely on that capital. Their cost changes with audience, timing, history, role, and culture.

Regeneration comes from reliability, generosity, preparation, and follow-through. The boring things are still the strongest foundation.

Avoid concealment. If you have a concern, state it honestly and professionally. Authentic is almost always cheaper than clever.

Going to zero after a worthy fight can be acceptable. Repeatedly spending past zero damages your ability to influence future outcomes.

Managers should use positional power to lower the cost of candor and protect people who raise responsible concerns.

Remember that the map is uneven. Bias and harmful cultures cannot be solved solely by better individual behavior.

Finally, play the long game. Build capital, spend it on outcomes that matter, learn from the result, and let your credibility regenerate.
